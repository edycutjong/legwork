Open by saying the name and one sentence: a standing payment that pays whoever runs it. Do not read the tagline. Thirty consecutive real executions on one order, one-second periods, on mainnet. On every single one, the number the contract computed for its own gas equalled the number the receipt reported — zero gas apart. The last five bars are the payee running its own payment; it is 2,500 gas cheaper because it is already warm, and the meter caught that too.
This is the situation every keeper-network sales page starts from. Recurring USDC on an EVM chain means paying gas in one token to move another. Either a network prices that through an oracle and bills an upkeep balance, or you run your own box with a hot key and a second-token balance that can hit zero while the dollars sit untouched.
On Arc the gas is the same asset as the deposit, so there is nothing to convert. The contract multiplies the gas it measured by the price it paid and sends that out of the order's own balance. That one line replaces the oracle, the upkeep token and the network. This is real: 58,415 gas at 20 Gwei on the first live run.
Fourteen lines, three of which matter. Record gasleft first. Pay the payee with a fixed stipend. Then measure — and after that point nothing variable happens: one value call, one fixed-width event. OVERHEAD is one calibrated constant for what sits outside the window. The price is capped at twice the base fee and at the payer's own ceiling, so an executor cannot drain a deposit by overpaying.
Stop here for a beat. Line two is what the contract paid the executor. Line four is what the transaction cost, taken from the receipt, not from us. They match to the wei. That is the entire claim, and the receipt beside it is how anyone checks it — the page decodes both in the browser, no backend.
This is the live page today: order 9 was created and executed this afternoon. Every number on it is an eth_call, a log or a receipt. A reviewer with a few cents of USDC on Arc can press Execute on order 1 right now and be paid the gas plus a cent. The two-minute video walks this exact path; it is linked from the BUIDL.
Be precise about the dependency. The trick works for any chain's native asset — an ETH-denominated order on Ethereum would behave the same. What does not survive the move is a USDC order, because there the gas and the deposit are different assets. Arc is where the native asset is the dollar people schedule payments in. The rest of Arc — unburned base fee, native transfer logs, finality at inclusion — makes it legible and safe, not possible.
Everything on this slide is a transaction hash or a command. The failure branches are not test cases only: each one happened on mainnet once. The refusing payee pauses the order and the executor is still paid. Executing early is a real status-zero receipt. Overpaying is a real receipt where the executor ate the difference. Clone, forge test, npm test, npm run recheck — about a minute.
Say these before anyone asks. Nobody outside the team has executed an order yet — the executors are our two wallets and the page button. The base fee never moved off 20 Gwei during the bench, so the two-times cap is proven by tests and one capped receipt. The explorer's verify API sits behind a challenge page; we check the deployed bytecode against the build instead.
The reusable part is the thirty lines between the first statement and the refund call. As an abstract contract with one modifier, any Arc function becomes permissionlessly runnable with the caller repaid from the contract's own USDC. That is where payroll, DCA into vaults, subscription pulls and agent jobs meet — with bots earning tips instead of anyone running a cron box.
The microgrant funds the modifier extraction and a public executor bot so the first outside executor exists. Office hours with the Arc team would settle two open questions: whether the 20 Gwei floor is a mainnet guarantee and how the validator set relates to the unburned base fee.
